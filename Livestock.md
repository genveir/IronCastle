# Livestock

Fetch this when animals come up. For the rules of caring for livestock, see `Work.md`.

## General

Livestock are obtained by finding them wild, trading for them, or through the narrative. However they arrive, they all work the same way. You must own an animal before you can Make a Promise about it.

By default adult livestock produce as soon as you get them. Like plants, animals mature very fast. Usually they will be adult from being a baby in a season.

Feed is assumed. The game is not granular enough to track hay.

**Livestock produce their resource all year round. Their season is when they produce extra.** In-season harvest gives +1 resource per filled box in Reap the Benefits, so a varied barn always yields a little extra.

Bees and other producers of sweet syrupy goodness count as livestock.

All livestock can be kept purely as companions. Make a Promise simply to spend time with an animal and take satisfaction as the reward.

### Blorb

Adorable little cousins to everyone's favorite: the slime. About a metre tall, total cuddlers, gentle and kind in nature but quick to run from trouble.

A blorb needs lots of water and food, often nutritional pellets. A happy, cared for blorb eventually grows a pom-pom on its head where it stores extra nutrients. A very happy blorb realises it has no use for the pom-pom and discards it.

In nature a discarded pom-pom is absorbed into the earth and feeds the underground root system that keeps the trees fed. At the farm it goes into cooking. The exterior is smooth and a little hard, the contents gooey and slightly sweet. Pom-poms are used as binding agents much like eggs, though the flavour makes them better for baking than frying.

### Nubelli

Distant relatives of the banamallard, as shown by the shared wobbly crest. Nubellis are birds, and unlike their cousins they can fly. They are so good at staying off the ground that some people believe they float.

A nubelli attracts moisture from the air and holds it around itself like a cocoon. Because of this they have been kept in houses and barns to fend off mold and mildew.

They eat fruit and bugs. Quiet alone, but a flock can be heard for miles, with a sharp melodious cry like the fine notes of a flute. A fully wrapped nubelli can be about a metre wide, hovering at the same height in the air. Its actual body is about half that, half a metre.

The cocoon is defence and nutrient storage, but nubelli cannot discard it, so they groom constantly. A rancher who grooms the cocoon regularly is a lifesaver, which is one reason nubelli have taken to farm life.

A picked piece of cocoon is dense and heavy like a wet sponge. Squeezed, it gives a healthy probiotic drink, blue melk, with the consistency of milk and a pleasant sky blue hue.

### Gnoseshroom

Nothing to do with mushrooms, gnomes, or noses. The gnoseshroom is a snail.

Its colourful spotted shell and nose-shaped horn led people to think it was a gnome. It was only recently that people realised its curious talent: the gnoseshroom can find the rarest of all truffles, the snuffle truffle, which sent the culinary world into a total snuffle truffle shuffle. Sightings are rare, especially in the wild.

They make good pets. They feed on moss and lichen and take nutrients from the soil beneath them. Low maintenance, especially for anyone who composts, since they love coffee grounds and kitchen scraps, though those are treats only.

Their favourite thing is walking in the shade, so expect to follow along. They are anything but slow.

### Prickly Dear

The people of the Valley were surprised to learn one of their local succulent species could move around.

They earned the name because most of them were too polite to move once someone started caring for them.

In the wilderness, prickly dears grow the fruit on their heads as a defence mechanism. The fruit, a syrupitaya, has large spikes to deter unwanted attention. They are about a metre tall and just as wide. Cared for at a ranch, the prickly dear still grows its signature spiky fruit, and discards it easily since it is safe.

Normally the inside of a syrupitaya is dense and fibrous, because the fruit focuses on hardening its spikes. When cared for and happy, the spikes are softer and pointless, and the inside is gooey and amber. An incredibly sweet syrup, the kind where a teaspoon sweetens an entire pot of coffee.

### Beaver Weaver

Short of stature, but they leave a mark that is anything but small. Their teeth and claws grow constantly and need grooming, which they do by breaking down bark and branches into a soft, wool-like material.

From a very young age they develop an affinity for weaving that material into a kind of yarn known as beave weave. It is easy to tell where beaver weavers have been: look for small piles of fibrous, natural balls of yarn.

People have used beave weaves for time immemorial, spinning them into thread and weaving them into cloth. Warm, if a little rough to the touch.

They are not opposed to ranch life and are particularly laid back, not asking for much food or shelter. They do require a lot of wood and branches to keep teeth and claws groomed. Many ranchers have found their beaver weavers eager to chew through fences, barn walls, and even front doors.

The ancient peoples of the Valley, who lived in harmony with the creatures of the forest, believed the entity overseeing the grand weave of fate was none other than a great and wise beaver weaver.

### Regurgeon

Strange little guys, standing just over a metre tall. Amphibious, so a good rancher keeps a covered pond for the midday dip.

Their body chemistry lets them hold nutrients and regurgitate them to feed their young. What they produce is not a mess but a beautiful, squishy "pearl."

These resemble salmon eggs, hence salmon pearls. They are not regurgeon eggs. Regurgeons are mammals. Salmon pearls are a super food for young regurgeons.

A happy regurgeon with plenty of water and food eventually makes enough internal nutrients to produce salmon pearls, though not for just anyone. Only for their young, or someone they believe is in need. Caring for a regurgeon makes them care for you in turn, which gives them a reason to grossly regurgitate a pearl for you, smiling up at you happily the whole time.

Salmon pearls are high in nutrients, very high in protein, and highly viscous inside, which is why they are often used in cooking as a substitute for eggs. Some people, particularly the fitness minded, swallow them raw.

### Pitcher Crab

No relationship in the valley is as enigmatic as that of the pitcher crab and the milk bees, two completely different organisms from different biomes sharing a curious symbiosis.

The pitcher crab's head crown is hollow and sometimes holds river water rich in minerals. The milk bees use that reservoir to store their pollen. The crab, aware of the bees' efforts, tries not to spill the contents of its crown, and in return the bees swarm predators to keep the crab safe.

Given time, the liquid inside the crown thickens into a nutritious nectar, which the crab offers to wounded and hungry creatures, or travellers, it stumbles upon.

Ranchers who keep pitcher crabs and their milk bees find them relatively easy. The crab is a herbivore and needs only a pond and plenty of plants. The bees need flowers for pollen. A farm close to a river is optimal.

Crab nectar can be used for almost anything: medicinal salves and ointments, cooking and baking, especially recipes calling for seafood flare. Because of its nutritious and soothing effects it has been a cold and throat remedy for generations.

### Chillachonk

SO FLUFFY. A metre and a half tall on average, though most of their incredible width is fluff. They can be either really chill or really, really hyper.

Incredible runners and jumpers. Some have been known to jump ten metres into the air, though it is better not to tell them they can do that. Fences lose all meaning at that height.

Over the year they continuously grow and groom their magnificent coats, leaving puffs of chilla wool stuck to bark or tumbling across the forest floor. Chillawool is incredibly soft and makes very warm clothes.

A happy chillachonk needs shelter from the weather, lots of hay and food pellets, and lots and lots of grooming. There is no happier chillachonk in the world than one being brushed. They are known to cry if you stop brushing for even a second, leaving young ranchers brushing away for hours.

### Banamallard

Colourful, easily excited, speedy large birds. Two and a half metres tall on average, with plumage so bright they can be spotted for miles.

The people of the Valley have always relied on banamallards for transport and as pack animals, though it would be a lie to call them domesticated. They do not see people as caretakers but as companions.

They expect to be paid for their efforts, likely proof of their symbiotic way of life. It is not rare to spot a banamallard in the wilderness helping a smaller critter cross a river, or helping dig a burrow or build a nest.

Strangely industrious, which is why they fit a ranch so well. People use them to get around faster and to do things like hoeing soil and picking fruit.

They love fruit and cannot get enough of it. They are supposed to eat grass and hay, but cannot do without fruit. Hungry banamallards have been known to feast on their rancher's orchards, so treat your mount before it treats itself to your harvest.

Banamallards are most infamous for their victory over the people of the Valley, in the event known as the Banamallard War. No people or banamallard were hurt. The cause was the lack of anything happening: a local merchant who used banamallards to pull wagons had been short-changing them on their food and fruit quota. Having had enough, the entire pack sat their colourful butts down and refused to get up until all banamallards were equally fed. When commerce ground to a halt the merchant made amends quickly. The people of the time, being a little melodramatic, called this the only war ever to shatter the peace of the Valley.

Ever since, it has been a popular saying that when you mess with one banamallard, you mess with the entire Valley.
